What do these controls do?
$ Value / % Change
Dollar value over time, or percentage moved from the pre-recession peak. Use % Change to compare recessions of very different sizes.
Nominal / Real
Real adjusts for inflation — what the money could actually buy. It matters most in the 1970s and early 1980s, when 1980's –17% nominal is far worse once ~13.5% CPI is applied.
Price Ret. / +Dividends
Adds dividend and interest income on top of price movement. Yields were much higher in earlier decades — around 5.5% on equities in the 1930s against ~1.5% in 2001. Interest on CDs and on Cash & Savings only shows up here, so a cash-heavy allocation looks flat in Price Ret.
Linear / Log
A log axis stops an outlier like the Great Depression flattening every other line into a band. Unavailable in % Change mode, where values cross zero.
Rebalance
Annual sells what held up and buys what fell, once a year, restoring your target weights — the discipline most investors find hardest in a crash.
Benchmark
Overlays the same starting capital held entirely in the S&P 500, on the same basis, so you can see whether diversifying actually helped. One recession at a time — with several selected the control greys out, and the comparison table below the chart carries the same comparison as columns for all of them.
Monthly withdrawal
Money taken out every month during the recession. Selling into a drawdown locks in losses the recovery never gives back — the sequence-of-returns problem.
Emergency fund
Cash held outside the portfolio and spent down before anything is sold, so it is not in your allocation, the donut or the Total Portfolio Value. Set a monthly withdrawal, then raise the fund and watch the drawdown get shallower — that difference is what the fund is worth. Once it runs out, the Cash & Savings bucket goes next, then everything else pro rata.

⚠ Methodology, Data Sources & Limitations

Corrections Applied to Source Data

1937–38 Recession
–49% → –54.5%
Bloomberg/Winthrop Wealth bear market tables (Jan 2023) confirm –54.5% from peak Mar 1937 to trough Mar 1938. Earlier –49% figure was an under-estimate.
1945 Recession
–16% → +38%
The only recession in history where equities rose. The S&P gained +38% during Feb–Oct 1945 as markets priced in the end of WWII — making this a unique anomaly in the dataset.
1948–49 Recession
–21% → –14%
The commonly cited –29.6% (Winthrop) measures from the May 1946 post-war peak — a 2.5-year window. The NBER recession itself ran Nov 1948–Oct 1949 and saw ~–14% within that window.

Documented Departures from the Proxy Rules

    Remaining 12 recessions are within 1–2 percentage points of Bloomberg/Winthrop figures. The 1953–54, 1960–61, and 1980 recessions did not trigger formal –20% bear markets; their drawdowns are NBER-window estimates from Shiller's price series.

    Primary Data Sources

    • S&P 500 drawdowns: Bloomberg bear market tables published by Winthrop Wealth (Jan 2023), cross-referenced with Robert Shiller's long-run S&P 500 price dataset (irrationalexuberance.com).
    • Recession dates: National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) official peak/trough dates.
    • Bond returns: Ibbotson long-term U.S. government bond total return series (1926+); Shiller's 10-year bond yield dataset used to derive pre-Ibbotson estimates.
    • Gold & Silver: Historical gold prices from Kitco / LBMA London Fix; silver from COMEX records. Gold was fixed at $35/oz under Bretton Woods until the Nixon Shock (Aug 1971), so pre-1971 gold returns are effectively zero.
    • REIT returns: NAREIT All Equity REITs index (1972+); real estate sector equities used as proxy for earlier periods.
    • International equities: MSCI EAFE index (1970+); U.S. equity used as a proxy for all pre-1970 periods where no reliable international index exists.
    • CDs: 3-month U.S. T-bill rate used as proxy throughout (CD rates track T-bills closely). FDIC insurance is assumed, so CDs show no principal loss.
    • Cash & Savings: Modelled as an interest-bearing high-yield savings account, not a checking account. The rate tracks each era's 3-month T-bill a little below the CD rate, but is capped at the Regulation Q ceiling for any recession before 1986 — federal law capped what banks could pay on retail savings deposits until deregulation completed that year, so the 12.5% available on a 1981 T-bill was not available on a 1981 savings account. Savers reached those rates through money market funds instead, which this tool does not model. FDIC insurance is assumed, so no principal loss.

    Structural Limitations

    • Recovery curves are modeled: The drawdown phase uses a cubic Hermite (S-curve) and recovery uses a square-root curve. Actual paths were irregular and non-monotonic — this is an approximation.
    • Dividends (user-selectable): Toggle total return including dividends via the Income control. Yields are era-specific: Large Cap ranged from ~5.5% in 1937–38 to ~1.5% in 2001–02; Bonds from ~2% (WWII era) to ~13% at the 1981–82 Volcker peak; REIT 9–10% in the 1980s to ~4.5% in 2020; CDs accrue each era's average 3-month T-bill rate (near zero in the 1930s–40s, ~12% at the Volcker peak), so CD interest appears only in +Dividends mode while principal stays flat. Cash & Savings accrues the same way at a high-yield savings rate — a little under the CD rate, and capped at the Regulation Q ceiling before 1986 — so a cash holding is flat in Price Ret. mode and earns interest only in +Dividends. Each month's income is the era yield applied to that asset's current balance and held as cash, not reinvested — so a position that has fallen 80% pays income on the reduced balance, not on its pre-crash value. Because the yield itself is held constant, income is understated in severe crashes (real yields rose as prices collapsed) and the figure should be read as a conservative floor.
    • No rebalancing assumed: Allocation percentages are held fixed throughout each simulation. Systematic rebalancing during a drawdown typically improves long-term results.
    • Nominal vs. real (user-selectable): Toggle inflation-adjusted values via the Returns control. Real mode deflates each path point by the recession's average CPI. Notable: 1980 (~13.5% CPI), 1973–75 (~9.2%), 1981–82 (~9.4%) — real losses were substantially worse than nominal. The Great Depression used –6.0% (deflation), meaning nominal losses slightly overstated real purchasing-power losses. Deflation is applied only through the downturn phase — the 1929–33 price decline did not persist through the multi-decade recovery, so it is not extrapolated past the trough.
    • Post-recovery growth assumption: Real mode keeps running after the nominal recovery window closes, because purchasing power can stay below baseline long after the nominal price level is restored. That extension assumes 7% nominal growth per year and long-run inflation normalising to 3%. Both are assumptions, not data, and they set how long Real mode reports a recovery taking — the 1973–75 real recovery is roughly five times the nominal figure largely because of them.
    • Assets that did not exist yet: An asset with no market in a given era (crypto before Jan 2009) is held flat at its notional value for that simulation rather than being dropped. It contributes no return, no yield and no inflation exposure, so it neither helps nor hurts — but it does dilute the rest of the portfolio, exactly as an equivalent cash holding would.
    • Withdrawal order: Withdrawals are taken in tiers rather than pro rata across everything, because the order is what an emergency fund is for. The Emergency fund is spent first and sits outside the portfolio entirely — it is excluded from the allocation, from every percentage and from the Total Portfolio Value, so it neither dilutes your weights nor flatters the reported drawdown. The Cash & Savings bucket goes next, since spending it realises no loss. Only then is the remainder sold pro rata, dividend cash before principal within each asset. The month the fund runs out is reported per recession: that is the month the portfolio starts absorbing the withdrawal. Real behaviour is messier — people cut spending in a downturn, and this models a fixed draw that never adapts.
    • Correlation simplification: Portfolio drawdown is the allocation-weighted average of individual asset class returns. Cross-asset contagion (e.g., 2008, when all risk asset correlations spiked to near-1) is not modeled.
    • One trough per recession: Each recession uses a single peak-to-trough figure. Multi-leg bear markets (e.g., the Dot-com bust had two distinct legs in 2001 and 2002) are collapsed to their aggregate drawdown.
    Not financial advice. This tool is for educational illustration only. Historical drawdowns varied significantly by sector, geography, and individual security. Your actual portfolio behavior in a recession will differ based on factors not modeled here: taxes, fees, margin calls, sequence-of-returns risk, and behavioral decisions made under stress.
